Fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) screens very small molecules, called fragments, instead of full drug-sized compounds. Fragments bind weakly — often only in the high micromolar to millimolar range — but efficiently, and they explore chemical space far better per compound tested.

The rule of three

Fragment libraries are often designed with the rule of three: molecular weight below 300 Da, calculated logP of 3 or less, and no more than 3 hydrogen-bond donors and 3 acceptors. Because fragments bind weakly, they are detected with sensitive biophysical methods such as X-ray crystallography, NMR or surface plasmon resonance, which also show where they bind.

Growing, merging and linking

  • Growing adds atoms to one fragment to reach new interactions.
  • Merging combines overlapping fragments into a single new core.
  • Linking connects two fragments that bind in neighbouring sites with a linker.

Why linking can pay off

When two fragments are joined, the combined molecule pays the cost of losing its freedom to move only once instead of twice. As a result, a well-linked molecule can bind much more tightly than the two fragments' affinities would suggest when added together — an effect called super-additivity.

The catch is geometry. The linker must hold both fragments in their original binding positions and orientations without strain. A linker that is slightly too long, too short or twisted can lose most of the expected gain.

Finding the right linker

  1. Start from the two fragments' 3D binding poses, ideally from crystal structures.
  2. Choose the attachment atoms on each fragment — usually hydrogens pointing towards each other.
  3. Search a library of linkers whose 3D ends match the distance and angles between those attachment points.
  4. Check that candidate products do not clash with the protein and are synthetically feasible, then refine and test.
