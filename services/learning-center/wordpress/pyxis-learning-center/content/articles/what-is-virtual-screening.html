--- title: What is virtual screening? topic: drug-discovery-basics order: 3 term: Virtual screening summary: Virtual screening uses computers to rank large compound libraries so that only the most promising molecules are bought, made or tested. objectives: - Distinguish ligand-based from structure-based virtual screening - Explain how enrichment is measured - Describe a practical virtual screening workflow ---

Virtual screening is the computational counterpart of high-throughput screening. Instead of testing every compound in a library, software scores the library and the team tests only the top-ranked molecules. A good virtual screen finds more active compounds per experiment than random selection.

Two families of methods

Ligand-based

If you already know at least one active molecule, you can search for compounds that resemble it. The usual tools are [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-a-molecular-fingerprint"]molecular fingerprints[/pyxis_learn_link] compared with [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-tanimoto-similarity"]Tanimoto similarity[/pyxis_learn_link], [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-substructure-search"]substructure search[/pyxis_learn_link], pharmacophore models and 3D shape matching. Ligand-based methods are fast — billions of comparisons are feasible — but tend to find molecules similar to what you already have.

Structure-based

If a 3D structure of the target is available, from crystallography, cryo-EM or [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-protein-structure-prediction"]structure prediction[/pyxis_learn_link], each compound can be [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-molecular-docking"]docked[/pyxis_learn_link] into the binding site and ranked by a scoring function. Docking can find chemically novel actives but is slower and its scores are noisy.

Measuring success: enrichment

Virtual screens are judged by enrichment: how many more actives appear in the top of the ranked list than chance would give. An enrichment factor of 10 at 1% means the top 1% of the list contains ten times as many actives as a random 1% would. Retrospective benchmarks use known actives mixed with "decoys"; the real test is always a prospective experiment.

A practical workflow

  1. Choose the library. In-stock compounds can be delivered in weeks; virtual (make-on-demand) libraries are far larger but must be synthesised.
  2. Filter. Remove compounds with reactive or assay-interfering groups and those outside the property range you need.
  3. Rank cheaply first. Use similarity or substructure search to reduce millions of molecules to thousands.
  4. Rank expensively second. Dock the shortlist and inspect the best poses by eye.
  5. Pick for diversity. Choose a varied set rather than the top 50 near-duplicates, then order and test.

In Pyxis Discovery, searches over stock and virtual small-molecule and macrocycle libraries feed directly into docking, so a shortlist can be ranked and ordered in one place.
