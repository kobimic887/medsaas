In 1997 Christopher Lipinski and colleagues analysed compounds that had reached clinical trials and proposed a simple guide for oral absorption, now known as the rule of five (Ro5). Poor absorption or permeation becomes more likely when a molecule has more than one of these:

  • molecular weight above 500 Da;
  • calculated logP (lipophilicity) above 5;
  • more than 5 hydrogen-bond donors;
  • more than 10 hydrogen-bond acceptors.

The rule (all its cut-offs are multiples of five) became a standard filter for screening libraries.

Drugs that break the rule

A notable set of approved oral drugs violates the Ro5 — many of them natural products, macrocycles or other large molecules targeting difficult proteins. Chemists call this region beyond rule of five (bRo5). Commonly quoted bRo5 ranges are roughly:

PropertyTypical bRo5 range
Molecular weight500 – ~1,000 Da
Calculated logPup to ~10
Hydrogen-bond donorsup to ~6
Hydrogen-bond acceptorsup to ~15
Polar surface areaup to ~250 Å²
Rotatable bondsup to ~20

How bRo5 drugs get absorbed

Size alone does not decide absorption. What matters is how polar the molecule appears when it crosses a membrane. Many successful bRo5 drugs are conformationally flexible in a controlled way: they form internal hydrogen bonds and fold to hide polar groups in fatty environments, then open up again in water. This behaviour is called chameleonicity and is discussed in [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-membrane-permeability"]membrane permeability[/pyxis_learn_link].

What this means for design

For bRo5 projects, simple 2D property filters are not enough. Teams measure permeability early, use 3D descriptors that account for conformation, and favour rigid, pre-organised scaffolds such as [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-a-macrocycle"]macrocycles[/pyxis_learn_link] over long floppy chains of the same weight.
