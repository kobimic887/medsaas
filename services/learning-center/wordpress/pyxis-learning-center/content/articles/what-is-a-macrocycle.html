A macrocycle is a molecule that contains a ring of at least twelve atoms. The ring can be built from amino acids (cyclic peptides), from polyketide chains like many natural antibiotics, or from fully synthetic building blocks.

Why macrocycles bind difficult targets

Many disease-relevant proteins act through large, shallow surfaces, for example where two proteins touch (protein–protein interactions). Conventional small molecules are too small to grip these surfaces strongly. Macrocycles are larger, so they make more contacts, and the ring pre-organises the molecule: it is already close to the shape it needs for binding, so it loses less flexibility (entropy) when it binds than an equivalent open chain would.

Closing a ring can also improve selectivity and metabolic stability, which is why medicinal chemists sometimes "macrocyclise" an existing open-chain lead.

The challenges

  • Drug-likeness. Most macrocycles fall outside Lipinski's rule of five. See [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-beyond-rule-of-five"]beyond rule of five[/pyxis_learn_link].
  • Permeability. Large polar molecules usually cross cell membranes poorly. Some macrocycles solve this by hiding their polar groups; see [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-membrane-permeability"]membrane permeability[/pyxis_learn_link].
  • Synthesis. Ring-closing steps can be low-yielding, so macrocycle libraries have historically been small.
  • Modelling. A big, flexible ring has many conformations, which makes 3D modelling and [pyxis_learn_link slug="what-is-molecular-docking"]docking[/pyxis_learn_link] harder than for rigid small molecules.
